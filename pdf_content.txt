 Executive Summary 
 Nigeria  is  the  fourth  largest  economy  in  Africa,  with  a  nominal  GDP  of  approximately  $300  billion 
 and  a  population  exceeding  200  million.  Yet  the  country's  productive  capacity  is  fundamentally 
 constrained  by  a  single,  persistent  failure:  the  inability  to  generate,  transmit,  and  distribute  adequate 
 electricity to its citizens, businesses, and institutions. 
 The  power  sector  deficit  sits  at  over  $100  billion  in  unmet  infrastructure  need.  The  national  grid 
 delivers  fewer  than  4  gigawatts  of  electricity  on  most  days  against  a  suppressed  demand  of  at  least 
 30  gigawatts.  Over  80  million  Nigerians  have  no  access  to  electricity  at  all.  Those  who  do  face  an 
 unreliable  supply  that  averages  fewer  than  12  hours  per  day  in  most  commercial  areas.  In 
 response,  the  private  sector  collectively  spends  an  estimated  $12  to  $14  billion  annually  on  diesel 
 generators  to  maintain  basic  operations.  That  expenditure  produces  no  productive  asset,  generates 
 no return, and compounds year over year. 
 This  report  makes  a  straightforward  case:  the  power  crisis  is  not  primarily  a  technical  problem.  It  is 
 a  capital  allocation  and  deal-structuring  problem.  The  technology  exists.  The  policy  frameworks  are 
 improving,  anchored  by  the  landmark  Electricity  Act  of  2023.  The  demand  is  unambiguous  and 
 backed  by  demonstrated  willingness  to  pay.  What  is  absent  is  the  structured  deployment  of  private 
 capital at scale. 
 This  report  identifies  five  investment  models  within  the  power  sector  that  are  viable  today, 
 executable  at  multiple  entry  scales,  and  capable  of  delivering  equity  returns  between  15  and  35 
 percent  depending  on  the  model  and  structure.  It  also  briefly  identifies  four  adjacent  sectors  where 
 energy  access  is  the  primary  constraint,  and  whose  productivity  gains  will  compound  the  returns 
 available to power investors. 
 If  Nigeria's  electricity  problem  is  solved,  it  does  not  merely  improve  one  sector.  It  unlocks 
 the  productive  capacity  of  every  other  sector  simultaneously.  Power  is  the  master  variable 
 in Nigeria's economic equation.

 The Power Sector: A Structural Diagnosis 
 2.1  The Generation Gap

 Nigeria's  installed  electricity  generation  capacity  stands  at  approximately  13  gigawatts  across 
 thermal  and  hydropower  plants.  In  practice,  the  grid  delivers  between  3,500  and  5,000  megawatts 
 on  any  given  day.  The  difference  between  installed  and  delivered  capacity  is  accounted  for  by  gas 
 supply  disruptions,  aging  infrastructure,  transmission  constraints,  and  persistent  commercial  losses 
 across the value chain. 
 The  government's  target  is  30  gigawatts  of  accessible  generation  capacity  by  2030.  Reaching  that 
 figure  requires  an  investment  of  over  $100  billion  across  generation,  transmission,  and  distribution 
 infrastructure.  Current  budgetary  allocations,  which  amount  to  approximately  5  percent  of  the  stated 
 annual  requirement,  make  public  funding  alone  categorically  insufficient.  Private  capital  is  not  an 
 option in this equation. It is a necessity.

 2.2  The Policy Turning Point: Electricity Act 2023 
 The  Electricity  Act  of  2023  is  the  most  consequential  regulatory  reform  in  Nigeria's  power  sector  in 
 two  decades.  It  decentralised  authority  over  electricity  markets,  empowering  state  governments  to 
 license,  regulate,  and  develop  independent  power  systems  without  federal  approval.  This  single 
 structural  change  removes  the  primary  bureaucratic  bottleneck  that  stalled  private  sector 
 participation for years. 
 State  governors  can  now  serve  as  direct  off-takers  for  power  purchase  agreements.  Lagos,  Rivers, 
 Kano,  Ogun,  and  Oyo,  all  of  which  have  significant  commercial  and  industrial  activity,  can  anchor 
 Private and ConfidentialPage  1 
 Nigeria Power Sector Infrastructure Report  |   Confidential  |   2025 
 solar  and  embedded  generation  projects  under  commercially  negotiated  terms.  This  creates  a  new 
 class of creditworthy counterparty for private developers and investors. 
 Complementing  the  Electricity  Act,  the  National  Integrated  Electricity  Policy  endorsed  in  2025  aligns 
 federal  and  state  priorities,  while  tariff  adjustments  in  2024  raised  Band  A  electricity  tariffs  to  N225 
 per  kilowatt-hour,  improving  revenue  collection  by  40  percent  within  the  year.  These  are  not 
 cosmetic reforms. They represent a structurally improved investment environment. 
 The  Electricity  Act  2023  changed  the  answer  to  the  question:  'Who  is  the  off-taker?' 
 Previously,  that  answer  was  a  single,  struggling  federal  utility.  Today,  it  can  be  a  state 
 government,  a  university,  a  hospital,  a  manufacturer,  or  a  commercial  estate.  The  risk 
 profile of the sector has fundamentally changed. 
 2.3  The Diesel Economy as Market Proof 
 Nigeria's  private  sector  spends  between  $12  and  $14  billion  annually  on  diesel-powered  generators. 
 At  an  average  cost  of  $0.35  per  kilowatt-hour,  this  is  the  most  expensive  form  of  electricity 
 consumption  in  any  large  economy  in  the  world.  It  is  also  the  clearest  possible  evidence  that 
 demand exists and that the market will pay. 
 Any  investor  offering  electricity  at  $0.10  to  $0.18  per  kilowatt-hour,  which  is  the  realistic  price  range 
 for  solar-generated  power  under  a  commercial  power  purchase  agreement,  is  immediately 
 competitive  with  diesel.  The  customer  does  not  need  to  be  convinced  to  switch.  The  switching 
 decision  is  self-evident  once  the  alternative  is  structured  and  available.  This  is  a  rare  investment 
 context:  the  demand  is  proven,  the  willingness  to  pay  is  established,  and  the  competing  product  is 
 more  expensive,  noisier,  more  polluting,  and  less  reliable  than  what  a  well-structured  solar  solution 
 can offer. 
 SECTION 03 
 Investment Models: Five Viable Entry Points 
 The  power  sector  is  not  a  monolithic  investment  proposition.  It  disaggregates  into  five  distinct 
 models,  each  with  a  different  capital  requirement,  risk  profile,  revenue  structure,  and  return 
 expectation.  An  investor  entering  this  space  does  not  need  to  build  a  power  plant.  There  is  a  viable

 entry  at  every  scale,  from  $200,000  to  $200  million,  and  each  model  has  documented  precedents  in 
 the Nigerian market. 
 Model 1  |  Solar Mini-Grids 
 Entry Scale: $500,000 to $5 million per site 
 A  solar  mini-grid  serves  a  defined  cluster  of  residential,  commercial,  and  institutional  customers  in 
 areas  where  the  national  grid  is  absent  or  unreliable.  The  developer  installs  solar  panels,  battery 
 storage,  and  a  local  distribution  network,  then  sells  metered  electricity  directly  to  end-users  at 
 regulated  tariff  rates.  Revenue  is  collected  via  prepaid  meters  and  payment  platforms,  eliminating 
 credit risk at the customer level. 
 The  Rural  Electrification  Agency  provides  licensing  and,  in  certain  cases,  viability  gap  funding  to 
 mini-grid  developers  to  improve  project  economics  in  lower-income  areas.  The  government's  target 
 of  achieving  30,000  mini-grid  projects  nationwide  by  2030  creates  a  well-defined  pipeline.  Nigeria 
 currently  has  fewer  than  200  functional  commercial  mini-grids.  The  gap  between  200  and  30,000  is 
 not a policy ambiguity. It is an investment opportunity. 
 •  Revenue:  Revenue  mode  is  metered  electricity  sales  to  residential  and  commercial 
 customers at N50 to N120 per kilowatt-hour 
 •  Scale:  Average  generation  capacity  per  site:  50  to  500  kilowatts  depending  on  community 
 size and demand density 
 •  Return:  Project  IRR  is  estimated  around  18  to  25  percent  depending  on  community  size, 
 REA viability gap funding, and operational cost management 
 •  Payback:  Payback period of 4 to 6 years 
 •  Primary  Risk:  Key  risk  is  customer  payment  discipline,  managed  through  prepaid  metering 
 and payment integration 
 The  proof  of  concept  for  this  model  exists.  Power  Africa's  programme  has  facilitated  over  1,005 
 megawatts  of  capacity  and  unlocked  $1.48  billion  in  investments  for  on-grid  and  off-grid  projects  in 
 Nigeria.  Multiple  developers,  including  Husk  Power  Systems,  Nayo  Tropical  Technology,  and  Rural 
 Spark,  have  demonstrated  viable  unit  economics  in  Nigerian  mini-grid  deployments.  This  is  not  an 
 experimental model. It is a replicable one. 
 Model 2  |  Commercial and Industrial Solar (Power Purchase 
 Agreements)

 Nigeria Power Sector Infrastructure Report  |   Confidential  |   2025 
 Entry Scale: $2 million to $50 million 
 The  Commercial  and  Industrial  solar  model  addresses  the  single  largest  direct  cost  of  doing 
 business  in  Nigeria.  A  developer  installs  rooftop  or  ground-mounted  solar  generation  at  a  factory, 
 hospital,  university,  retail  centre,  or  industrial  estate,  then  sells  the  electricity  generated  to  that 
 facility  under  a  long-term  Power  Purchase  Agreement  at  a  rate  below  what  the  facility  currently  pays 
 for  diesel-generated  power.  The  customer  saves  money  immediately.  The  developer  earns  a 
 contracted cash flow for 10 to 25 years. 
 This  model  entirely  bypasses  the  national  grid.  It  does  not  depend  on  distribution  companies,  does 
 not  require  wheeling  agreements,  and  does  not  expose  the  investor  to  grid  instability.  The  risk 
 profile  is  anchored  to  the  creditworthiness  of  the  corporate  off-taker,  not  the  government  or  the  utility 
 sector.  For  a  large  bank,  a  cement  manufacturer,  a  supermarket  chain,  or  a  pharmaceutical 
 company, the credit risk is materially different from sovereign risk. 
 •  Pricing:  Electricity  price  to  customer  between  $0.10  to  $0.16  per  kilowatt-hour,  compared  to 
 $0.30 to $0.40 per kilowatt-hour for diesel 
 •  Contract:  Contract  structure  of  10  to  25  year  Power  Purchase  Agreement  with  creditworthy 
 corporate counterparty 
 •  Return:  Project  IR  of  18  to  24  percent;  leveraged  returns  can  exceed  30  percent  with 
 favourable debt structuring 
 •  Market:  Addressable  market  is  every  manufacturer,  bank,  hospital,  hotel,  and  educational 
 institution  currently  running  a  diesel  generator,  estimated  at  over  60  million  generators 
 nationally 
 •  Payback:  Payback period of 4 to 7 years 
 The  Commercial  and  Industrial  solar  model  is  the  cleanest  risk  proposition  in  Nigeria's 
 energy  sector  today.  The  customer  is  a  business  that  already  pays  for  electricity.  You  are 
 simply  offering  it  to  them  at  a  lower  price  from  a  more  reliable  source.  No  subsidy  required. 
 No  government  dependency.  No  currency  mismatch  if  contracts  are  structured  in 
 dollar-equivalent terms. 
 Model 3  |  PAYGO Home Solar Systems 
 Entry Scale: $1 million to $20 million (portfolio financing) 
 Pay-as-you-go  solar  financing  solves  the  access  problem  at  the  household  level.  A  company 
 provides  a  solar  home  system  comprising  panels,  battery  storage,  and  basic  appliances  to  a 
 household  in  exchange  for  small,  regular  payments  made  via  payment  apps.  The  system  is 
 remotely  monitored  and  can  be  deactivated  if  payments  lapse,  eliminating  the  moral  hazard  of 
 Private and ConfidentialPage  1 
 Nigeria Power Sector Infrastructure Report  |   Confidential  |   2025 
 non-payment.  Once  the  customer  completes  payment,  typically  over  12  to  36  months,  they  own  the 
 system outright. 
 For  investors,  the  proposition  is  a  receivables  portfolio.  Investing  in  a  PAYGO  solar  company  means 
 financing  the  receivables  generated  by  household  payment  plans  across  thousands  or  hundreds  of 
 thousands  of  customers.  The  aggregated  receivables  are  predictable,  diversified,  and  secured 
 against  an  asset  that  can  be  deactivated  and  recovered.  The  model  has  been  successfully 
 implemented  at  scale  in  Kenya,  Tanzania,  and  Rwanda  by  companies  such  as  M-KOPA  and  d.light, 
 and is expanding in Nigeria. 
 •  Revenue:  Revenue  mode  is  monthly  repayments  from  household  customers  via  payment 
 platform, typically N3,000 to N8,000 per month 
 •  Return:  Target IRR for receivables investors is around  20 to 28 percent 
 •  DFI  Access:  Development  Finance  Institution  appetite  such  as  DFC,  IFC,  and  Norfund  have 
 all financed PAYGO portfolios in sub-Saharan Africa 
 •  Blending:  Blended  finance  opportunity  for  First-loss  tranche  from  DFIs  reduces  effective  risk 
 and improves returns for commercial investors 
 Model 4  |  Metering Infrastructure (Distribution Company Partnerships) 
 Entry Scale: $1 million to $30 million 
 Nigeria's  11  electricity  distribution  companies  collectively  under-meter  their  customer  base  by  an 
 estimated  40  to  60  percent.  Unmetered  customers  are  billed  on  estimated  consumption,  which 
 suppresses  collection,  enables  energy  theft,  and  prevents  accurate  load  management.  The  Meter 
 Asset  Provider  regulatory  framework,  introduced  by  the  Nigerian  Electricity  Regulatory  Commission, 
 enables  private  companies  to  finance,  install,  and  own  electricity  meters  that  are  then  leased  to 
 distribution companies under shared-savings agreements. 
 A  Meter  Asset  Provider  installs  prepaid  smart  meters  at  customer  premises.  The  distribution 
 company  collects  metered  revenue  and  remits  a  monthly  lease  payment  to  the  Meter  Asset 
 Provider.  The  federal  government  has  committed  to  installing  1.1  million  meters  in  the  near  term 
 under  the  National  Mass  Metering  Programme.  The  total  metering  gap  is  estimated  at  over  7  million 
 connections.  The  gap  between  government  programme  targets  and  total  need  is  the  investment 
 pipeline. 
 •  Revenue:  Revenue  model  is  a  monthly  lease  payments  from  distribution  companies  per 
 meter installed

 •  Return:  Target  IRR  is  around  16  to  22  percent  in  naira  terms,  with  hedging  mechanisms 
 available through indexed contracts 
 •  Policy:  Government  supports  through  National  Mass  Metering  Programme  provides  partial 
 subsidy for low-income meter deployment 
 •  Payback:  Payback period is around 3 to 5 years 
 Model 5  |  Utility-Scale Independent Power Projects 
 Entry Scale: $50 million to $500 million+ 
 Utility-scale  Independent  Power  Projects  are  the  conventional  infrastructure  investment  model  in 
 which  a  private  developer  constructs  and  operates  a  large  generation  facility,  selling  output  to  a 
 utility,  state  government,  or  large  industrial  off-taker  under  a  long-term  Power  Purchase  Agreement. 
 Nigeria  has  commissioned  several  utility-scale  projects,  including  the  Azura-Edo  IPP  and  the  Kainji 
 hydropower  privatisation.  The  Jigawa  Solar  PV  initiative,  currently  in  development,  targets  50  to  100 
 megawatts in its initial phase with long-term expansion to 1,000 megawatts. 
 Utility-scale  projects  carry  higher  capital  requirements  and  longer  development  timelines,  but  also 
 attract  the  deepest  pool  of  institutional  capital,  including  pension  funds,  sovereign  wealth  funds,  and 
 major  DFIs.  The  African  Development  Bank,  IFC,  and  the  US  International  Development  Finance 
 Corporation  have  all  provided  debt  and  equity  financing  to  Nigerian  IPPs.  Blended  finance 
 structures,  in  which  DFIs  absorb  first-loss  risk,  materially  improve  the  economics  for  commercial 
 equity investors. 
 •  Return:  Target  equity  IRR  is  around  14  to  18  percent  for  well-structured  projects  with 
 creditworthy off-takers 
 •  Structure:  Debt  financing,  typically  60  to  70  percent  leverage  from  DFIs  and  commercial 
 banks 
 •  Timeline:  Development  timeline  estimated  around  24  to  48  months  from  financial  close  to 
 commercial operation 
 •  Risk:  Key  requirement  is  a  Bankable  Power  Purchase  Agreement  with  sovereign  guarantee 
 or creditworthy state government as off-taker

 SECTION 06 
 Risk Considerations and Mitigants 
 No  investment  report  on  Nigerian  infrastructure  is  complete  without  an  honest  account  of  the  risks. 
 The  following  risks  are  material,  well-understood  by  experienced  market  participants,  and 
 manageable through appropriate deal structuring. 
 •  Currency  Risk:  Currency  risk  is  the  primary  concern  for  US  dollar  investors.  Naira 
 depreciation reduces the dollar value of naira-denominated revenues. 
 •  Mitigant:  Structure  Commercial  and  Industrial  PPAs  in  dollar-equivalent  terms  indexed  to  the 
 official  exchange  rate,  or  pair  investments  with  dollar-revenue  generating  assets  such  as 
 export-facing businesses.

 •  Counterparty  Risk:  Counterparty  risk  exists  where  government  entities  or  distribution 
 companies are the off-taker. 
 •  Mitigant:  Prioritise  creditworthy  private  sector  counterparties  at  entry  stage;  access  World 
 Bank partial risk guarantees for projects with government off-takers. 
 •  Regulatory  Risk:  Regulatory  and  policy  risk  remains  despite  improvements.  The  regulatory 
 environment,  while  materially  better  than  five  years  ago,  can  shift  with  changes  in 
 administration. 
 •  Mitigant:  Engage  international  arbitration  clauses  in  all  project  agreements;  partner  with  DFIs 
 whose participation serves as a political risk deterrent. 
 •  Execution  Risk:  Operational  execution  risk  in  Nigeria's  environment  requires  experienced 
 local partners. 
 •  Mitigant:  Do  not  attempt  to  operate  remotely.  Build  or  partner  with  local  project  management 
 capability from the outset. 
 SECTION 07 
 Conclusion 
 Nigeria's  power  sector  deficit  is,  at  its  core,  a  capital  deployment  problem.  The  technology  is 
 available.  The  demand  is  proven  beyond  reasonable  doubt.  The  regulatory  environment  has 
 undergone  its  most  significant  reform  in  two  decades.  The  returns  available  to  structured  private 
 capital are among the highest of any infrastructure asset class globally. 
 What  remains  is  the  architecture  of  the  deal:  identifying  the  right  investment  model  for  the  available 
 capital,  structuring  agreements  that  appropriately  allocate  risk  between  private  investors  and  public 
 sector partners, and building the operational track record that unlocks institutional capital at scale. 
 This  report  is  a  first-principles  framework  for  that  architecture.  It  is  intended  to  be  a  working 
 document,  not  a  static  study,  and  is  designed  to  support  ongoing  investor  conversations,  deal 
 origination, and fund formation activity in Nigeria's energy infrastructure market.
